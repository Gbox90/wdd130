Food waste • cost • climate • community

When food is wasted, everything behind it is wasted too.

Food in the trash represents more than a forgotten leftover. It also represents the money, water, energy, labor, transportation, farmland and packaging used to get that food to Central Florida homes, restaurants and stores.

Local response

Orlando is already trying to keep food out of landfills.

The City of Orlando maintains food-waste education and composting resources and encourages residents to buy only what they need, reuse what they have and compost or otherwise divert scraps from landfill disposal.

The city has also received a USDA Composting and Food Waste Reduction grant for an off-grid, solar-powered community composting facility using a static aerated system designed to process food waste efficiently.

The national picture

The environmental cost continues after food is thrown away.

31%
of the U.S. food supply

ReFED's 2024 U.S. Food Waste Report estimates that nearly one-third of the U.S. food supply is lost or wasted across the food system.

ReFED report ↗
58%
of fugitive landfill methane linked to wasted food

EPA research estimates that landfilled food is responsible for 58% of methane emissions escaping from municipal solid-waste landfills, even though food is only part of the material buried there.

U.S. EPA ↗
30–40%
of U.S. food supply historically estimated uneaten

This range appears across federal and food-waste research and is central to Wade's discussion of inconsistent date labels and the national effort to reduce wasted food.

Wade (2023) ↗
Why methane matters: food decomposes rapidly in oxygen-poor landfill conditions. Much of that decomposition happens before landfill-gas collection systems can capture the gas, which is why prevention, donation and diversion are generally preferred over disposal.
Household behavior

Some of the most preventable waste happens at home.

Date labels can cause edible food to be discarded too soon.

Wade (2023) examines food date-labeling standards as a major policy opportunity. The problem is not simply that people ignore labels; inconsistent wording can make quality guidance look like a food-safety deadline.

Website teaching point: “Best if Used By” generally communicates quality, while true safety-based handling guidance should be treated differently. Always pair date-label education with safe food-storage guidance.

NRDC's household findings

NRDC's Wasted report describes households as the largest food-waste-generating sector in the data it reviewed. Its three-city household research found that a substantial share of discarded food was edible and highlighted produce, leftovers and date-label confusion as important household issues.

Waste has upstream impacts

Throwing away the food does not erase what it took to make it.

Money

Households lose the purchase value of food that is never eaten. Businesses also absorb purchasing, storage, preparation and disposal costs.

Water & energy

Food production requires irrigation, processing, refrigeration, cooking and transportation. Those inputs are consumed even when the final food is discarded.

Land & materials

Agricultural land, fertilizer, feed, packaging and transportation capacity are committed before food ever reaches the plate.

Landfill methane

When food is buried, anaerobic decomposition generates methane. EPA research identifies food as a disproportionately important source of landfill methane emissions.

Disposal capacity

Keeping organics out of the waste stream preserves landfill capacity and reduces the volume local systems must collect and process.

Lost community value

Edible surplus has a much higher social value when it can safely feed people than when it becomes garbage. Prevention comes first; safe donation is another high-value pathway.

From awareness to action

A simple hierarchy for households.

01

Prevent

Plan portions, shop with a list, use what is already at home and avoid buying more perishables than the household can realistically eat.

02

Rescue

Turn leftovers into planned lunches or a second dinner. Freeze food while it is still safe and appealing rather than waiting until it is about to spoil.

03

Share or donate safely

Businesses and organized events can use established food-rescue partners when safe, allowable surplus food is available.

04

Compost unavoidable scraps

Peels, cores and other unavoidable scraps can return nutrients to soil through appropriate composting instead of becoming landfill material.
